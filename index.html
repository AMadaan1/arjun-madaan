I build and evaluate machine learning systems for medicine — benchmarking what clinical language models actually get right, and designing models that learn from physiological signals when data is scarce.

Currently

Two things have my attention. The first is measurement: medical language models are being evaluated on exam questions, which is not the same as being evaluated on the job. I built MCAIT, a ten-task benchmark that scores models on diagnostic reasoning, patient interaction, ethics and scientific knowledge, including simulated patient conversations, and ran it across 37 models.

The second is architecture. Clinical time series are noisy, irregularly sampled and badly imbalanced — the event you care about is rare, which is exactly the condition under which ordinary networks fail. I'm working on physics-informed liquid neural networks for early sepsis prediction, where a continuous-time model with physiological constraints built into its loss should need less data to get to a useful answer.
